## Purpose

The scoring framework, and the scale alignment between latent and estimated quantities. `rhome_eval` is the only package permitted to see both the engine's estimates and the simulator's truth — which makes it the last place the discipline of the whole build can quietly fail.

## The finding: every latent correlation reported so far was wrong

The harness's first output showed **directional accuracy of 43%** on all three channels — worse than a coin flip at saying whether fatigue was rising or falling. No misspecification produces that. Something structural had to be wrong.

The engine's day-N state is captured at the **close** of day N, waking plus sixteen hours, after that day's session. The simulator records its latent state **on waking**, before it. Pairing them index for index compares two states sixteen hours and one training session apart, which inverts the day-to-day differences almost perfectly.

| Channel | As reported at L2.5, the Layer 2 audit and Checkpoint 2 | Actual |
|---|---:|---:|
| Local fatigue | +0.450 | **+0.949** |
| Neuromuscular | not reported | **+0.956** |
| Systemic | +0.388 | **+0.973** |
| Directional accuracy | 43% | **95%** |

Per model muscle group, correlations run **+0.956 to +0.981**.

The engine reconstructs latent state far better than anything in this build log has claimed — but see the re-audit below, which is where that sentence gets its proper size.

### The part worth dwelling on

The wrong numbers came with a confident explanation attached. I wrote, more than once, that the modest correlations were *modest by design* — citing the inverse-crime audit's finding that the engine's single exponential cannot represent the simulator's two-component recovery, and adding that a correlation near 1 would be evidence of an inverse crime rather than of success.

Every clause of that is true about the model. It was being used to explain an artefact.

A plausible reason attached to a wrong number is worse than no reason, because it closes the question. The number survived a stage report, a 46-check audit and a checkpoint gate, and what eventually caught it was not scrutiny of the figure but a *different* metric that could not be explained away: 43% is below chance, and nothing about misspecification makes a model worse than guessing.

**Corrected in place**, with the error recorded rather than quietly overwritten: `L2.5_session_driver.md`, `LAYER2_AUDIT.md`, `CHECKPOINT_2.md`, `tools/layer2_audit.py`, and the driver tests, whose own comparison had the same fault.

## Three protections, built in rather than remembered

**The engine runs before any truth is read.** `run_engine` and `attach_truth` are separate calls, so nothing in the engine's path could consult a latent series even if someone tried to make it. Mechanically nothing would break if truth were loaded first; the split exists because a harness holding the answers while the engine works is one refactor away from using them.

**The alignment is fitted once and frozen.** One scalar per channel, least squares through the origin — an intercept would let a constant error be absorbed into the alignment and disappear. Fitted on development, content-addressed, and applied unchanged to the holdout. Attempting to fit one *on* the holdout raises.

**The holdout is sealed, and openings are recorded.** Opening requires a stated reason and writes to `data/holdout_openings.json`. A second opening is permitted — forbidding it outright only encourages working around the guard — but every result then carries the mark, because a number from a twice-opened holdout is a weaker claim and should say so.

## Findings

### The harness leaked its own holdout through its test suite

The seal was checked in `evaluate()`, the convenience wrapper. `run_engine` had no guard, and two tests in this file promptly used it to run the engine across all 210 holdout days in order to exercise an unrelated code path.

Nothing was reported from those runs and no holdout metric was computed, so no real damage was done. But the shape of the failure is exactly the one this project keeps finding: **a guard that covers only the polite entry point is not a guard.** The check now sits on `run_engine`, and the two tests were rewritten to use hand-built runs that touch no sealed day.

### A stronger baseline than the ladder had

The ladder now includes mean-of-last-5, which on the pooled sample beats the engine by more than median-of-last-3 did — MAE 0.0408 against the engine's 0.0447. Adding the weakest plausible baseline and declaring victory is a way of losing on purpose, so the ladder carries the strongest simple predictors, not the most flattering.

### Every metric refuses a sample too small to mean anything

`pearson` over three points returns `None`, not 1.0. This is the vacuous-pass failure found three times already in this project, and putting the refusal in the primitive means no caller can reintroduce it.

## Re-audit: how much of +0.95 is actually the model

A correction that turns +0.45 into +0.95 deserves the scrutiny the low number should have had. Three adversarial checks, at the athlete's prompting.

### 1. Is the offset principled, or the best-scoring lag?

| Lag | Local fatigue | Neuromuscular | Systemic |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| −1 | +0.163 | −0.092 | +0.074 |
| 0 | +0.447 | +0.277 | +0.387 |
| **+1** | **+0.949** | **+0.956** | **+0.973** |
| +2 | +0.518 | +0.296 | +0.441 |

Sharply peaked, and independently justified by the timestamps rather than by the scores: the engine's day-N state sits **8 hours** from the simulator's day N+1 and **16 hours** from its day N. The right pairing was already determined before any correlation was computed.

### 2. Would a broken engine score the same?

| Predictor | Local fatigue | Neuromuscular | Systemic |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| **The engine** | **+0.949** | **+0.956** | **+0.973** |
| Count working sets, decay at 30 h | +0.911 | +0.873 | +0.945 |
| Count working sets, decay at 8 h | +0.750 | +0.819 | +0.802 |
| Count working sets, decay at 120 h | +0.751 | +0.617 | +0.736 |
| Trained yes/no, decay at 30 h | +0.597 | +0.702 | +0.663 |
| Total repetitions, decay at 30 h | +0.876 | +0.766 | +0.897 |

**A single accumulator counting working sets scores +0.911 to +0.945.** It knows nothing about exercise, repetitions, effort, muscles, or recovery context. The engine's advantage over it on the aggregate channels is **+0.029 to +0.083**.

Almost all of a headline correlation is *"did you train recently, and how much"*. Both series are driven by the same training log, so a high correlation of levels is close to free. The same finding as the performance ablation at Checkpoint 2, one stage later and by the same route: a number reported without a reference point.

Differencing does not rescue it — day-over-day change correlates +0.986 for the engine and +0.966 for the counter, because both track the same training rhythm.

### 3. What the engine is actually for

The counter has no way to tell chest from quadriceps. It must give every muscle the same series.

| | Engine | Counter |
|---|---:|---:|
| Mean across 16 model muscle groups | **+0.966** | +0.520 |
| Worst muscle | +0.942 (abs) | +0.230 (anterior delts) |
| Best muscle | +0.981 (anterior delts) | +0.760 (quads) |

**Advantage +0.446**, and the engine's worst-tracked muscle beats the counter's best by a wide margin.

This is the number worth reporting, and it is also the one the product depends on: the body map is per muscle, not an aggregate. On rest days, where only the decay model acts, the engine leads +0.93 against +0.87 — a real but modest margin, which is the honest size of the recovery model's contribution.

### What this changes

The engine is good, and better than this log has said. But *"correlates +0.95 with latent fatigue"* is close to meaningless as a claim, and I would have written it. The defensible claim is **per-muscle attribution**, where a trivial predictor cannot follow.

`state_baselines.py` now makes the comparison structural: a latent-state figure arrives with what a counter would have achieved beside it, and a test fails if the engine's aggregate advantage is ever quietly reported as though it were the whole result.

## Notes carried forward

- Scale alignment fits one scalar per *channel*, not per muscle. Per-muscle alignment would fit sixteen nuisance parameters and flatter the per-muscle RMSE; L3.2 should report per-muscle correlation, which needs no alignment, and reserve RMSE for the aggregate.
- Residual autocorrelation of +0.274 is unexplained. It could be the warm-up transient, since consecutive early observations share a lagging capacity estimate, or it could be structure in the fatigue model. L3.3 separates them.
- `peak_timing_error` and `crossing_timing_error` are built and unit-tested but not yet used against a run; L3.2 is where they earn their place.
