## The conditioning, working

The same wearable reading, read against two different training histories:

| Context | ρ | Training explains |
|---|---|---|
| After a hard session | 0.9224 | 47% of the HRV depression |
| The same reading on a rest day | 0.8019 | 0% — entirely unexplained |

That distinction is what a rolling baseline alone cannot make, and it is the whole reason for the change.

---

## Validated against known truth

The simulator knows what actually drove each night's HRV, so the partition can be checked rather than assumed.

**The training component is removed.** Correlation between the HRV deviation and systemic load falls from **−0.323 raw to −0.031 after conditioning**, with 10.3% of the variance stripped. That is the partition's direct claim, and it holds.

**The Grade C coefficient is close to the truth it was never fitted to.** The engine assumes a systemic load one standard deviation above normal depresses HRV by 0.30 standard deviations. Regressing the simulator's own output recovers a slope of **0.33**. The prior was chosen from a reported direction and an estimate of plausible magnitude, and lands within roughly a tenth of the relationship the simulator generates.

That is not proof the coefficient is right for a human — the simulator is a model too. It is evidence the guess was not wild, and it is exactly the check personalisation will later replace with a fitted value and an interval.

## Findings

### A test whose premise was wrong while the code was right

The first validation asked whether the conditioned residual correlates *better* with the simulator's unobserved stress process than the raw deviation does. It failed: 0.422 against 0.428.

Diagnosing it rather than adjusting γ found the reason. Load and stress are themselves slightly correlated in the simulator (−0.097), so the load term was **inflating** the apparent stress correlation. Conditioning strips that spurious help at the same moment it reduces variance, and the two effects nearly cancel.

The conditioning was working the whole time. The test was asking the wrong question — an indirect one, contaminated by a confound — where a direct one was available: does the residual still carry the training component? It does not.

The general lesson is worth keeping: when a validation fails, the first question is whether it measures what it claims to. Tuning a coefficient until an indirect test passes would have moved a nearly-optimal parameter away from the truth in order to satisfy a badly-posed question.

### A documented gap: how thin is too thin for a baseline

`MODEL.tex` fixes the 42-day window but does not say how few observations are too few. A standard deviation from three nights is not a baseline, and a channel standardised against one would swamp the multiplier through sampling noise alone.

The minimum is the engine's choice, registered with an explicit `unverifiable_reason`. Below it a channel contributes zero — the same treatment the specification gives missing data, which is the conservative reading.

---

## Decisions

**A reading is standardised against the days before it, never a window containing itself.** `step()` computes the context and then folds the day into the baselines, in that order. The reverse would let today's value soften its own deviation.

**Unexplained deviation moves confidence, never ρ.** The multiplier carries evidence; the surprise is reported separately. An engine repeatedly surprised should say so rather than quietly acting as though it understood.

**Temperature enters through its absolute deviation.** Departure in either direction is unfavourable, which is what `MODEL.tex` specifies and is easy to lose when transcribing a signed coefficient.

---

## Deviations from specification

None. The baseline minimum fills a gap `MODEL.tex` leaves open, and is recorded as the engine's choice.
