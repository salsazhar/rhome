## Why both columns are there

Each of the first five is a **matched pair** — two constructed sessions differing in one thing — pushed through the engine and, separately, through the simulator's own physiology. Reporting both orderings is what makes this an evaluation rather than a restatement of the engine's dose table:

- both correct → the ordering is a property of both models and the engine has it right;
- they disagree → the engine is wrong about the world this project models, and the margin says by how much;
- **neither produces it** → the specification is asking for something no model here does, which is a finding about the specification.

The third case is the one a test comparing the engine against its own assumptions could never surface, and it is the reason the truth column exists.

---

## The pattern: correct, and consistently compressed

The engine expresses **67% of the true contrast on average**, and never more than 88%. Every ordering is understated; none is overstated.

That is the misspecification the inverse-crime audit documents, appearing where a user would feel it. The simulator amplifies differences the engine cannot represent — state-dependent accumulation, a volume-by-failure interaction, a two-component recovery split — while the engine is linear in set count and separable in effort and repetitions.

For the product this means the *direction* of every comparison is trustworthy and the *size* is conservative. A hard session reads as harder than an easy one, by less than it truly was. Of the two ways to be wrong, that is the better one, but it should be stated rather than discovered.

A test now requires every ratio to lie strictly between 0 and 1, so an ordering that ever became overstated would fail rather than pass quietly.

### In plain terms

Two sessions: 5×5 squats to failure, and 5×5 squats stopped three reps short. Same exercise, same sets, same reps — only the effort differs.

| | To failure | At 3 RIR | Ratio |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Engine | 1.458 | 0.972 | **1.50×** |
| Simulator | 1.811 | 0.969 | **1.87×** |

The engine says going to failure costs **50% extra** quad-and-glute fatigue. The simulator says **87%**. So the engine sees 58% of the real penalty — the "expressed" column.

The raw numbers are not comparable across rows: the engine and simulator use different internal units, and 1.458 against 1.811 means nothing on its own. Only the ratio *within* each row carries information, which is why the table is entirely ratios.

For the athlete: when the app says today was harder than yesterday, trust the direction. The size is conservative — the real gap is larger than shown.

### The athlete's response, and the decision it produced

Raised immediately: the engine *should* produce curvature, because that is an innate property of fatigue. Correct, and `MODEL.tex` half-concedes it — "non-linear fatigue accumulation" appears in the list of properties *the simulator* must have so the two are not algebraic mirrors. The linearity was an inverse-crime precaution taken during development, not a physiological claim.

Specified as **L5.3**, to land after Checkpoint 3 against a frozen v0 baseline, using a structurally different form from the simulator's — amplification by position within the session rather than by current state. Adding it now would invalidate every number L3.1 to L3.4 produced and leave nothing to compare against.

## Findings

### Three measurement errors in one metric

The sixth case cannot be built from a matched pair, because suppression is monotone in fatigue by definition — a hand-built low-fatigue state would out-project a high-fatigue one whatever the engine did. It has to be measured from the run, and I got the measurement wrong three times.

1. **Wrong window direction.** Taking the *latest* projection after the boundary put the reading well into the next accumulation block, by which time the rebound was spent. Measured: engine −0.06% against a true +1.47%.
2. **Wrong truth quantity.** Comparing against *neutral* capacity, which a deload is not supposed to move. §19.3 asks about expected *performance*. Measured: true "gain" of −1.00%, so the specification appeared to fail on its own terms.
3. **Wrong boundary.** Comparing the end of the deload against the start of the next block, when the claim is about the fatigued state *before* the deload against the fresh state after. Measured: engine +0.22% against a true −0.13%.

Each version produced a number, and each had an available story: *the engine does not model deload rebound*. The thing that stopped all three being written down was checking what the specification actually says before accepting what the metric returned — D19, applied to my own instrument rather than to the engine.

`LatentSeries` gained an `expressible` series as a result, and the distinction between neutral and expressible capacity is now explicit in the code rather than something to remember.

---

## Decisions

**The failure-versus-non-failure case is matched on set count**, five sets against five, so the ordering cannot be explained by volume. A test asserts the match, because an unmatched pair would make the case trivially true.

**Ranking cases are constructed, not mined from the dataset.** Matched pairs do not occur naturally in a training log, and picking the nearest available pair would introduce whatever else differed between them.

None. All six orderings in §19.3 are tested.

## Notes carried forward

- The 67% compression figure is measured on constructed pairs and on this simulator. It is not a claim about the real world; it is a claim about how much of *this simulator's* structure the engine reproduces.
- The deload rebound agrees on sign in 34 of 49 pairs — 69%. The remaining 31% are pairs where one or other moved the wrong way, and it is not known whether those are noise or a subset with something in common. L3.7 should look.
